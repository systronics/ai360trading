Position Sizing in One Formula: How Many Shares Should You Buy?

The rule to remember
Rule: shares equal the money you can lose divided by the loss per share, and keep the risk to about 1 percent of the account or less.

The lesson, in words

Most traders decide how many shares to buy by feel, or by how much money they have. Professionals do it the other way round. They first decide how much they can afford to lose on the trade, and the number of shares follows from that.

The formula is one line. Number of shares equals the money you are willing to lose on this trade, divided by the loss per share if your stop is hit. The stop decides the size, not your excitement.

A worked example. Your account is $ 100000. You risk 1 percent per trade, so you can lose $ 1000. A stock trades at 100 and your stop is 5 below the entry. The loss per share is 5. So 1000 divided by 5 gives 200 shares, a position worth $ 20000.

Now change only the stop. With a tight stop of 2, the same $ 1000 of risk allows 500 shares, worth $ 50000. With a wide stop of 10, it allows only 100 shares, worth $ 10000. In all three cases, if the stop is hit, you lose $ 1000. The risk stays fixed, and the position size adjusts.

What this does not prove. Sizing protects you, but it does not create profit, and it does not guarantee the stop will fill at your price: gaps and slippage can make a real loss bigger than planned. Rule: shares equal the money you can lose divided by the loss per share, and keep the risk to about 1 percent of the account or less. This is education, not investment advice.
