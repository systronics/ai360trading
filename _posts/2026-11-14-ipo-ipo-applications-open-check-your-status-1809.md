## The Belief  
“IPO Applications Are Open — Here’s How to Check Your Status” is a line you see on every brokerage dashboard. Many Indian investors believe that once an IPO opens, a quick click guarantees a windfall. The belief is simple: get the allotment, sell on day one, and watch the profits roll in. I hear it at family gatherings, on WhatsApp groups, and in every market‑talk podcast. The story feels airtight, especially when friends brag about a 30 % listing gain on a recent tech issue.  

## What the Numbers Say  
If you strip away the hype, the data tells a different story. Across the last decade, the average first‑day premium for Indian main‑board IPOs hovered around 10 %‑12 %. However, a deeper dive shows that roughly 60 % of those issues underperform the broader market within the first twelve months. The grey market premium (GMP) – the unofficial price at which the issue trades before listing – often spikes, but it rarely predicts long‑term returns. In the United States, the average post‑IPO three‑year return for S‑&P‑500 constituents is roughly 7 % per annum, barely beating a diversified equity basket bought at the same time.  

## Where It Is True  
The belief holds water in a narrow context. If you’re a seasoned trader with a tight risk appetite, a well‑priced IPO can act as a short‑term catalyst. A high GMP can signal strong demand from institutional investors, especially when foreign institutional investors (FIIs) file sizable block deals. In those cases, a disciplined trader can capture a modest listing gain and exit before the inevitable price correction.  

Also, the mechanics of the application are transparent. In India, the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) system guarantees that your funds stay in the bank until the allotment is finalized. No money disappears into a black‑hole. That safety net is real and valuable for a beginner.

## Where It Fails  
The myth crumbles when you look at the average retail experience. Most first‑time IPO investors allocate only a fraction of their portfolio, often because the allotment process is random. Even if you get an allotment, the listing day can be a roller‑coaster driven by speculative buying, not fundamentals.  

Promoter quality, use‑of‑funds, and realistic valuation are frequently ignored. A recent Indian IPO saw a GMP of ₹45, yet the company’s balance sheet revealed a net‑loss of ₹800 crore and a cash burn rate that would deplete its runway in 18 months. The hype around the GMP pushed many retail investors to apply, only to see the share price tumble 25 % within weeks.  

Globally, the same pattern repeats. The March 2020 crash showed that even high‑profile IPOs can be hammered when macro sentiment turns sour. A tech firm that listed with a 40 % GMP in early 2020 saw its share price lose half its value within two months as investors fled risk assets. The lesson is clear: a high GMP does not guarantee durability.  

## A Simple Test You Can Run  
Before you hit “Apply”, run this three‑step sanity check.  

1. **Financial Health Scan** – Pull the last three years of profit‑and‑loss statements. Compute the net‑profit margin. If the margin is negative or below 5 %, ask yourself whether the business model is proven or still experimental.  

2. **Valuation Ratio Check** – Compare the issue price to the company’s FY‑2025 EBITDA. A price‑to‑EBITDA (P/EBITDA) above 25 × in an Indian non‑financial firm usually signals an over‑priced issue. In the US, a P/E above 30 for a mature consumer‑goods company is a red flag.  

3. **Promoter & Use‑of‑Funds Map** – List the top three promoters, their past ventures, and any regulatory blemishes. Then, read the “Use of Funds” section line by line. If more than 60 % of the proceeds are earmarked for “working capital” without a clear growth plan, the IPO is likely a cash‑pump.  

If any of these checkpoints raise doubts, pause. The test takes less than five minutes but can save months of regret.  

## The Bottom Line  
I’m not saying IPOs are dead. They remain a vital channel for companies to raise capital, and for investors who do their homework, they can be a useful addition to a diversified portfolio. But the shortcut narrative – “apply, get allotted, sell on day one, and profit” – is a myth that leads many to over‑expose themselves.  

The real edge lies in disciplined application and rigorous evaluation. Treat the IPO like any other equity investment: assess the balance sheet, check the valuation, and understand the promoter’s track record. Use the ASBA system in India or a brokerage platform in the US/UK to submit your application, but don’t rely on GMP as a crystal ball.  

A controversial take: I think the market’s obsession with first‑day gains is a distraction that benefits brokers more than investors. The data on post‑listing performance is stark – most issues underperform the index within a year. If you’re chasing a quick win, you’re essentially gambling on market sentiment, not company fundamentals.

## Quick Answers  

**Q1: How to apply for IPO online India ASBA UPI step by step?**  
A: 1) Log into your broker’s portal (e.g., Zerodha). 2) Navigate to the “IPO” section. 3) Choose the issue and select “Apply via ASBA”. 4) Enter the number of shares and your PAN. 5) Authenticate the transaction using UPI or net‑banking. 6) The amount gets blocked in your bank account until the allotment is announced.  

**Q2: What is IPO GMP grey market premium and its real limits?**  
A: GMP is the price at which an IPO trades unofficially before listing, usually quoted in rupees per share. It reflects demand from a small, unregulated market. The premium can swing wildly – sometimes exceeding 100 % of the issue price – but it rarely translates into sustainable post‑listing returns. Regulatory bodies warn that GMP is not a reliable indicator of valuation; it’s more a barometer of speculation.  

**Q3: Should I hold an IPO after the listing gain or sell immediately?**  
A: It depends on your investment horizon and the fundamentals you uncovered in the three‑step test. If the company shows solid earnings, a reasonable valuation, and a clear use‑of‑funds plan, consider holding for at least 12‑18 months to let the market digest the information. If the fundamentals are weak and the GMP was the only driver, an early exit might be prudent.
